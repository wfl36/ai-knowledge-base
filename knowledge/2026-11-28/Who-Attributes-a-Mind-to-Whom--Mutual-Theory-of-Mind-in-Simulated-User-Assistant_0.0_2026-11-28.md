## 项目描述
arXiv:2611.33327v1 Announce Type: new Abstract: When users assume that an AI chatbot has mental states, they are more likely to develop over-reliance, emotional dependence, and in extreme cases, psychological harm or self-harm. However, how these mind attributions arise in conversations and how human or AI factors influence this is still unknown. We study how users attribute minds using a minimal framework: a simulated user with different characteristics chats with assistants that differ in social style and memory. We also introduce a message-level measure of who attributes a mind to whom in four directions, checked with trained coders and applied by a fine-tuned encoder that reproduces an LLM judge. Our results show that, on average, nine out of thirteen characteristics raised simulated users' attribution to the assistant. We found that relational needs such as attachment anxiety raised it as theory predicts, some general personality traits raised it as well, and AI-specific characteristics the least. By contrast, the assistant's social style barely changed simulated users' mind attribution. Instead, sycophantic instructions raised the assistant's attribution to itself. Memory raised users' attribution only with some user models. The model playing the user changed overall attribution and the effects of characteristics, style, and memory. Our framework measures how persona descriptions affect mind attribution in simulation. It gives concrete hypotheses for human studies and shows the need for more consistent user simulators.
